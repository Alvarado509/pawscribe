How to Write a SOAP Note

A practical guide for veterinarians: what goes in each section, a worked example, and the mistakes that make notes hard to use.

Documentation is a cornerstone of veterinary practice. It keeps care continuous, keeps the team on the same page, and protects you legally. There are 130,415 veterinarians in the US, and 93.9% of vet practices are independently owned — most of them small teams where one clear note has to do the work of a whole handover.

It is also where the day goes. Vets spend 2–3 hours per day on documentation, and a single SOAP note takes 10–15 minutes to write by hand. A good note is not just thorough — it is structured so the next person can read it in seconds.

What is a SOAP note?

SOAP stands for Subjective, Objective, Assessment, and Plan. It is a structured method for documenting a patient visit, in a fixed order, so anyone on the veterinary team can quickly understand the patient’s status, your thinking, and what happens next.

A well-written SOAP note gives you clear communication across the team, continuity of care across visits and across vets, a legal record of what you found and decided, and a clear basis for accurate billing.

S: Subjective — what the owner tells you

The Subjective section captures information from the client’s perspective: what they observed and how they describe the problem.

  • Chief complaint: the main reason for the visit, ideally in the owner’s own words — for example, “limping since yesterday.”
  • History of present illness: onset, duration, frequency, severity, what makes it better or worse, and any treatment already tried.
  • Review of systems: a brief check of other body systems — appetite, thirst, urination, defecation, coughing, sneezing, vomiting, diarrhoea, energy, behaviour.
  • Environment and routine: diet, exercise, housing, travel, contact with other animals, recent changes.
  • Medications and preventatives: everything the pet receives, including flea and tick and heartworm preventatives, with doses and frequency.
  • Vaccination history: as reported by the owner, or confirmed from previous records.

What does not belong here: your own exam findings, test results, interpretations, or treatment recommendations. If you observed it, it goes in Objective. If you concluded it, it goes in Assessment.

O: Objective — what you find

The Objective section holds factual, measurable, observable data collected by the veterinary team — verifiable and free of interpretation.

  • Physical exam findings: nose-to-tail, system by system. General appearance, mentation, body condition score (BCS), hydration, vital signs — temperature, pulse, respiration, mucous membrane colour, capillary refill time — and each system: eyes, ears, mouth, heart, lungs, abdomen, urogenital, musculoskeletal, neurological, skin, lymph nodes.
  • Diagnostic results from this visit: in-house lab results, imaging findings, cytology, or external lab results received during the appointment.
  • Measurements: weight and anything else you measured.
  • Behavioural observations: what staff observed — for example, “tense during abdominal palpation” or “bright, alert, and responsive.”

What does not belong here: owner-reported history (Subjective), your interpretation or problem list (Assessment), or treatment plans (Plan).

A: Assessment — what you think it is

The Assessment is your professional interpretation of the Subjective and Objective findings. It answers “what is wrong?”

  • Problem list: a concise, prioritised list of active problems. For a healthy patient, that can simply be “healthy adult canine.”
  • Differential diagnoses: the possible causes you are considering for each problem, in order of likelihood.
  • Working diagnosis: your most likely diagnosis from the evidence so far.
  • Prognosis: a brief expected outcome for each problem.
  • Justification: one line tying the diagnosis to specific S and O findings.

What does not belong here: new history or exam findings, detailed diagnostic plans, or vague statements with no clinical justification.

P: Plan — what you are going to do

The Plan is your strategy for managing the patient’s problems. It answers “what are we going to do about it?”

  • Diagnostic plan: further tests needed to confirm or rule out your differentials.
  • Therapeutic plan: treatments, medications, and procedures — with dose, route, frequency, and duration for every medication.
  • Client education: the instructions you gave the owner, in plain terms.
  • Follow-up: when to recheck, what the owner should watch for, and what needs an emergency visit.
  • Preventative care: vaccines, parasite control, diet, and lifestyle changes.
  • Referral: if a specialist is needed.

What does not belong here: repetition of information already stated in S, O, or A, or anything unrelated to this visit.

Worked example: routine dog wellness exam

This is an example note, not a real patient. Names are placeholders.

S — Subjective

Patient: [Patient name], 5-year-old male neutered Labrador Retriever. Reason for visit: annual wellness exam.

Owner reports the dog is doing well at home. Eating and drinking normally. Urinating and defecating without issues. Good exercise tolerance — daily walks and weekend trips to the dog park. No vomiting, diarrhoea, coughing, sneezing, or lameness. Currently on a monthly flea and tick preventative and a monthly heartworm preventative. Vaccines due: DHPP, leptospirosis, rabies. Owner is concerned about bad breath.

O — Objective

Weight: 32.5 kg, unchanged from last year. BCS: 5/9. Temperature: 101.5°F. Pulse: 90 bpm, strong and regular. Respiration: 24/min. Mucous membranes pink, capillary refill time under 2 seconds. Bright, alert, and responsive, good body condition, clean coat.

Head, eyes, ears, nose, throat: eyes clear, no discharge; ears clean, no odour or pain; mouth shows mild gingivitis and moderate dental tartar, mainly on molars and canines; lymph nodes normal. Heart: normal sounds, no murmur. Lungs: clear, no cough on tracheal palpation. Abdomen: soft, non-painful, no organ enlargement. Musculoskeletal: normal gait, no lameness, no joint pain. Skin: healthy coat, no lesions or parasites. Neurological: normal reflexes, proprioception, and gait.

Diagnostics today: faecal flotation negative; heartworm antigen test negative.

A — Assessment

1. Healthy adult canine — overall excellent health on history and exam.
2. Dental disease, early stage — consistent with mild gingivitis and moderate tartar. Prognosis excellent with treatment.

P — Plan

Administered DHPP, leptospirosis, and rabies vaccines. Reviewed upcoming preventative due dates. Recommended dental scaling and polishing under anaesthesia, with pre-anaesthetic bloodwork first, and discussed at-home dental care afterwards. Gave vaccine-reaction instructions (watch for lethargy, facial swelling, or vomiting) and reviewed adult nutrition. Recheck for the dental procedure; annual wellness again in one year.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Mixing sections. The most frequent error. Owner reports go in S, exam findings in O, your interpretation in A, future actions in P.
  • Vague language. “Dog had an upset stomach” tells the next vet nothing. Write “owner reports one episode of vomiting and soft stool yesterday evening.” Quantify where you can — lameness grade, BCS, temperature.
  • Skipping pertinent negatives. “No coughing,” “no heart murmur detected,” and “no lameness” are findings too. Document what you did not find.
  • An Assessment that does not follow from S and O. If you diagnose ear disease, the S and O sections should show the scratching and the inflamed ear canal that led you there.
  • An incomplete Plan. “Meds given” is not a plan. Dose, route, frequency, duration, client instructions, and a recheck date are.
  • No problem list. Even a healthy patient deserves one line: “healthy adult canine, no active problems.”
  • Skipping sections on “normal” visits. Every section still applies — the Assessment is just short.
  • No date or signature. Every note needs both.

Frequently asked questions

What does SOAP stand for?

Subjective, Objective, Assessment, and Plan. It is a structured way to document a patient visit: what the owner reports, what you find on exam and testing, your interpretation, and what you plan to do.

What goes in the Assessment section of a SOAP note?

Your professional interpretation of the Subjective and Objective findings: a prioritised problem list, differential diagnoses, your working diagnosis, and a brief prognosis. No new history or exam findings belong here.

Do I have to write all four sections for a routine wellness exam?

Yes. Even for a healthy patient, address every section. The Assessment can simply state that the patient is a healthy adult, and the Plan covers vaccines, parasite prevention, and the next visit.

How long does a SOAP note take to write?
